Vibe coding (describing what you want in plain words and letting a model write
the code instead of typing it yourself) has crept into how a lot of people
start their first projects. With a portfolio the temptation is big: in a couple
of afternoons you already have something to show, instead of spending weeks
fighting with CSS. But like any shortcut, it's worth thinking about what you
gain and what you lose before betting everything on it.

## Pros

**Trying things stops hurting.** Trying a completely different look (from
eighties neon to something minimal, from a synthwave grid to a flat design) no
longer eats a whole afternoon of hand-written CSS. It takes a few prompts. And
that changes more than it seems: you can throw out an idea that isn't working
without feeling like you're throwing out hours of work, and you get to the
version that fits sooner.

**It's easier to start.** Sitting down in front of a blank editor with no idea
where to begin is one of the biggest things that holds back anyone who is
learning. Sitting down in front of a chat and saying what you want to make,
much less. And a portfolio often stalls at "I'll do it once I know more", so
that difference decides whether the project starts or stays in your head
forever.

**You practice something else.** Not writing every line, but reading what
someone else wrote and deciding whether it makes sense. When you learn to code
on your own you almost never practice that, and it also changes the usual
question: it's no longer "can I write this?" but "can I tell if this is
written well?" It's an uncomfortable question, honestly.

## Cons

**It can hand you code you don't understand, if you don't ask.** A model
doesn't warn you when something is delicate. A navigation menu closing with the
Escape key, giving focus back to whoever opened it, Tab not escaping to hidden
links: none of that usually comes out right the first time unless you ask for
it. And if you don't know it's missing, you don't ask. Vibe coding doesn't
replace knowing what to ask.

**Changing your mind gets too cheap.** When trying a new look costs one prompt
instead of one afternoon, it's very easy to spend weeks fiddling with the look
of something that's still empty inside: one background after another, one font
after another, while the important part, the projects and what you can really
do, still isn't written. Changing the visuals became easy. Writing the content
costs what it always did.

**The question of who did the work matters more on a portfolio than on any
other project.** A portfolio is the proof you show that you can build
something. If in an interview someone asks why you picked a certain color or
how an animation works, you need to be able to answer for real, not repeat
whatever the model left in a comment.

## Where I land

Not for it or against it, really. For trying things fast, and for starting
when the blank page feels too scary, vibe coding works great. As a substitute
for understanding what's underneath, it's a disaster, and the line between the
two is thinner than it looks at first.

If you're going to build your portfolio this way, my advice is simple. Read
every change before accepting it. Ask "why" more often than you feel you need
to. And don't settle until you can explain any line as if you had written it
yourself, because in an interview, the one who answers for that project is you.
